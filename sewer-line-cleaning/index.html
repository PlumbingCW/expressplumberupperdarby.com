Main line clog or branch clog

A branch clog affects one fixture or one bathroom. A main line clog affects everything that drains below a certain level, and the lowest drains show it first, usually a basement floor drain, a first floor toilet or a laundry tub. If the lowest fixtures back up and nobody has run water upstairs, the main is blocked.

Calling right away matters, because every flush adds to the backup. If you are unsure, tell the dispatcher which fixtures are affected, and we will tell you whether it sounds like the main.

How a main line is cleaned

  1. 1

    Find the cleanout

    Most houses have an access point at the foundation or in the basement.

  2. 2

    Cut the blockage

    A large machine with a cutting head opens the line.

  3. 3

    Scour if needed

    A jetter cleans the pipe wall when grease or scale is the cause.

  4. 4

    Check with a camera

    A look inside confirms the line is clear and shows why it clogged.

When the line is open, we explain what we pulled out. A mat of roots, a clump of wipes and a layer of grease all point to different fixes. If roots are the cause, a repair or lining may be needed, which the sewer line repair page explains.

What gets into a main line that should not

Flushable wipes are the leading cause of avoidable clogs, because they do not break down like toilet paper. Paper towels, feminine products, cooking grease and cat litter also lead to blockages. Tree roots, though, are the cause of most chronic main line clogs in older housing.

Mature street trees have decades to find a joint in an older clay or cast iron lateral. If your main clogs every year or two, ask for a camera look so you know whether the answer is a better cleaning schedule or a real repair.

Keeping the line clear

  • Flush only toilet paper and human waste.
  • Pour cooled cooking grease into a container and throw it away.
  • Know where your cleanout is and keep it accessible.
  • If you have had two clogs in a year, schedule a camera look.

For a scheduled look at the pipe itself, see sewer inspection, and for stubborn buildup the hydro jetting page explains the stronger method.

Signs the main line, not a fixture, is the problem

  • More than one drain is slow at the same time.
  • A basement floor drain gurgles or backs up when you flush upstairs.
  • Toilets bubble when the washer drains.
  • A sewage smell appears in the basement without a visible leak.

Any one of these on its own may be a fixture problem. Two or three together almost always point to the main. The sooner it is cleared, the less likely sewage is to reach finished floors, and the less likely you are to deal with cleanup that insurance may or may not cover.

Access, cleanouts and what we need to start

Most houses have a cleanout, a capped pipe near where the sewer leaves the building. If it is accessible, we open it and run the cable or jetter from there. If there is no cleanout or it is buried, we may pull a toilet to reach the line, and we explain that before we begin. Installing a proper cleanout is worth discussing, because it makes every future cleaning faster and cheaper.
