Sump Pump Discharge Line Repair for Upper Darby, PA Homes

The discharge line is the pipe that carries water from your sump pump out of the house, and when it fails, the pump may run all day without keeping the basement dry.

This guide explains the common failures and what a repair looks like.

How the discharge line works

The pump pushes water up through a pipe with a check valve, through the basement wall and away from the foundation. The check valve keeps water from flowing back down into the pit. The pipe outside ends at a spot where water can flow away without returning to the house.

If any part fails, the pump may cycle too often, make noise or run without lowering the pit.

Frozen outletPump runs, water backs up in the pipeThaw, clear, add a freeze-resistant outlet

Discharge too close to the houseWater returns to the foundationExtend the pipe

To test, pour water into the pit and watch the outlet. If water arrives quickly and flows away from the house, the line is working. If it trickles or stops, there is a restriction or a break.

Where the water should go

Discharge water should flow away from the house to a place where it cannot flow back. It should not be sent into the sanitary sewer, because stormwater overloads the system. Local rules apply to where it can go, and a neighbor's property is not an option. We explain the options for your lot before we pipe anything.

If you have no pump yet, or yours is old, see sump pump installation and repair, and for wider moisture problems see basement waterproofing.

Winter and the discharge line

In winter, an outlet at ground level can freeze closed or fill with ice, and the pump will push water against a plug. Water that cannot leave the pipe may leak at the joints or force the pump to work too hard. A freeze-resistant outlet or a pipe that slopes to drain when the pump stops lowers the risk.

  • Keep the outlet clear of leaves, snow and ice.
  • Make sure the pipe slopes away from the house, with no low spots that hold water.
  • Insulate exposed pipe where it makes sense.
  • Check the outlet after a cold snap.

Signs the discharge line needs attention now

  • A wet or muddy patch in the yard along the pipe route.
  • Water spraying or dripping at a joint near the wall.
  • A pump that cycles every few seconds even in dry weather.
  • A gurgling sound as water falls back through the pipe.

Any of these can show up in the middle of a storm, which is the worst time to find out. Walk the line on a dry day and look for sections that sag, crack or sit under soil that has washed away.

If you have to replace the line, rigid PVC with solid glued joints is common for the indoor part, with a short flexible section where it passes outside if the manufacturer allows it. Keep the number of elbows small, because each one adds resistance and gives debris a place to catch.

Water, creeks and local rules around Upper Darby

Cobbs Creek runs along Upper Darby Township's eastern border, and Darby Creek runs through the surrounding area. Heavy storms can raise groundwater, but we do not say that any particular street floods. Ask the township about rules for discharging pump water near streets, sidewalks and storm drains.

Upper Darby Township has adopted the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code and requires permits for residential alterations and repairs. Permit rules vary by job, so ask the township Department of Licenses and Inspection what applies to yours.

Upper Darby Township Code Chapter 265 says plumbing contractors working in the township must employ a licensed master plumber, and only the master plumber can apply for plumbing permits. Plumbing licenses in the township expire on March 31 each year.

Repair steps

  1. Turn off power to the pump and unplug it.
  2. Check the check valve and the pipe above it for cracks and clogs.
  3. Trace the pipe outside to find the leak, the freeze or the low spot.
  4. Replace damaged sections with the right pipe and fittings, glued or sealed as required.
  5. Restore power, fill the pit and watch the pump and the outlet.

Before the next storm

Test the pump and the discharge line on a dry day. A pump that works but has nowhere to send water is no help. If the discharge line is old, buried or cracked, replace it before it fails, not after the basement floods.

Sources: the pump maker's guidelines set discharge size and check valve use, and the International Plumbing Code (IPC) limits where stormwater may be sent.
