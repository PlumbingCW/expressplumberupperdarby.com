Sewage backup: what to do first

  1. Stop using toilets, sinks, showers and appliances that drain.
  2. Keep people and pets away from affected floors.
  3. Do not use chemical cleaners in a backed-up line.
  4. Photograph the damage for your records.
  5. Call us on the 24/7 line and describe which fixtures are affected.

We often clear the line first to restore service, then use a camera to see why it failed. That order stops the backup quickly and gives us the footage needed to decide on a repair. See sewer line cleaning for the clearing step and sewer inspection for the camera work.

What actually goes wrong with a sewer lateral

The lateral is the pipe that carries waste from the house to the main in the street. It can fail in several ways, and the right repair depends on which one the camera shows.

Root intrusion

Roots enter at joints and grow into a mat that catches everything.

Cracked or offset pipe

Ground movement or age cracks a joint or shifts a section.

Bellied pipe

A low spot holds water and solids, causing repeat clogs.

Collapsed section

The pipe has failed completely and no flow passes.

Pipe lining for older homes

Pull in place pipe lining uses a resin-saturated liner pulled through the old pipe and cured to form a new pipe inside the old one. It avoids trenching across a lawn, a driveway or a patio, which matters on small lots with mature plantings. It does not suit a pipe that has collapsed, and some lines need cleaning and a camera pass before lining is possible.

Cast iron and clay lines in older Upper Darby housing

Homes from the early and middle twentieth century often have cast iron inside the house and clay tile outside. Clay joints admit roots and shift over decades, while cast iron scales and cracks. In older, tree-lined neighborhoods such as Drexel Hill or Aronimink, roots are a frequent finding on camera.

Before any digging, ask the Department of Licenses and Inspection in Upper Darby about permit and street-opening rules, because they vary with where the line runs. We explain what is typical and what the township decides.

Questions to settle before any repair starts

  • Where exactly is the damage, and how was it found?
  • Is the pipe collapsed, cracked or only restricted by roots?
  • Which parts of the line are mine, and which belong to the township or the sewer authority?
  • What does each option cost, and what restoration is included?
  • What permits does the work need, and who applies for them?

A repair quote that skips these answers is a guess. We put them in writing after a camera inspection, and we are clear about where the lateral becomes the township's concern. Before any digging, a call to 811 has underground utilities marked, and we make sure it happens.
