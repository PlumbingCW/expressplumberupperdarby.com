Water Heater Anode Rod Replacement in Upper Darby, PA

The anode rod is a sacrificial metal rod inside a tank water heater that corrodes so the steel tank does not.

Checking and replacing it is one of the few maintenance jobs that can extend a tank's life, and this guide explains how.

What the anode rod does

Inside a steel tank, a glass lining protects most of the surface, but tiny gaps remain. The anode rod is made of magnesium, aluminum or zinc and corrodes faster than steel. As it wears away, it takes the corrosion that would otherwise attack the tank. When the rod is gone, the tank starts to rust.

The rod usually screws into the top of the tank, and some heaters have a combination of a rod and a hot water outlet fitting. Electric and gas tanks both have them. Tankless heaters do not.

Types of anode rod

MaterialGood forWatch out for
MagnesiumMost water, best protectionWears faster in soft water
AluminumHard waterAluminum salts can build up
Zinc-aluminum blendReducing sulfur or rotten egg smellCheck the heater manufacturer's guidance
Powered anodeLong-term protection without a sacrificial rodNeeds power and costs more

Ask what the manufacturer recommends. Some warranties depend on using an approved rod.

How to check the rod

  1. Turn off the gas or the electric breaker to the heater.
  2. Close the cold water supply and open a hot faucet to relieve pressure.
  3. Drain some water from the tank, enough to lower the level below the rod.
  4. Use a socket and a long breaker bar to unscrew the hex head on top of the tank. This takes real force, and an impact wrench helps.
  5. Pull out the rod and look at it. If it is mostly eaten away or has bare steel showing, replace it.

Many rods are stuck. In tight spaces such as a low ceiling basement, there may be no room to pull a full-length rod straight up, and a segmented rod can be used. This is a common reason people call a plumber.

When to replace

  • Inspect the rod every few years, sooner if the water is hard or you have a softener.
  • Replace it when it is thin, pitted or mostly gone.
  • Replace it earlier if you notice rust-colored hot water.
  • A heater past ten to twelve years old may not be worth the effort, because the tank may be corroded already.

If the tank is leaking, an anode rod will not fix it. Our water heater repair page explains what we can repair, and water heater replacement explains when it is time for a new tank.

Tools and cost of doing it yourself

You need a socket that matches the rod head, usually one and one sixteenth of an inch, plus a long breaker bar and a hose to drain the tank. A flexible segmented rod helps in low basements. Plumber's tape on the threads and a bucket for water make the job cleaner.

The rod itself is not expensive compared with a new tank, which is why it is worth checking early. Call a plumber if you are not sure the job is safe, if the rod is stuck or if the tank already leaks around the fittings. A plumber can do it in a short visit and also check the relief valve, the vent and the connections while the tank is drained.

Keep notes on the date, the type of rod and what you found. Next time, you will know how fast the rod wears in your water and when to look again.

Water softeners and anode rods

Softened water can make an anode rod wear faster, so a house with a softener may need to check the rod more often. A water softener is also something we install, as covered on the plumbing installation page.

Sediment and a flush

When you drain part of the tank to reach the rod, take the chance to flush sediment too. Draining a few buckets of water from the bottom valve until it runs clearer helps the heater work more efficiently and makes less noise. If the water comes out with a lot of grit, the tank has built up scale.

Do not leave the heater on while the tank is low. Electric elements burn out if they are not covered with water, so always refill the tank fully before you restore power.
